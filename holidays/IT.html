Italy's public holidays are almost entirely rooted in the Catholic liturgical calendar, blended with a few 20th-century civic dates like Liberation Day and Republic Day that mark the country's transition from monarchy to republic after the Second World War. Beyond the national list, every Italian town also observes its own patron saint's feast day as a local holiday, so the full holiday count in Italy depends heavily on the city. What Each Holiday Means
January 1, 2026Thursday
Capodanno
The first day of the Gregorian calendar year, marked with fireworks, family gatherings, and a fresh start after the festive season.
January 6, 2026Tuesday
Epifania
Marks the visit of the Three Wise Men to the infant Jesus, celebrated in Italy with the gift-giving figure of La Befana.
April 5, 2026Sunday
Pasqua
The most important date in the Christian calendar, celebrating the resurrection of Jesus Christ.
April 6, 2026Monday
Lunedì dell'Angelo
The day after Easter Sunday, traditionally used to extend the Easter weekend into a four-day break.
April 25, 2026Saturday
Festa della Liberazione
Commemorates Italy's liberation from Nazi occupation and fascism at the end of the Second World War in 1945.
May 1, 2026Friday
Festa del Lavoro
Honours workers and the labour movement, held nationwide on 1 May.
June 2, 2026Tuesday
Festa della Repubblica
Celebrates the 1946 referendum in which Italians voted to become a republic, ending the monarchy.
August 15, 2026Saturday
Ferragosto o Assunzione
Known in Italy as Ferragosto, a Catholic feast that doubles as the peak of the summer holiday season.
October 4, 2026Sunday

November 1, 2026Sunday
Tutti i Santi

Immacolata Concezione
A Catholic feast honouring the Virgin Mary, widely observed with Mass and family time.
